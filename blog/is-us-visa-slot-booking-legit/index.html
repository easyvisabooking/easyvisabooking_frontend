Are US Visa Slot Booking Services Legit? An Honest Answer

Some are ordinary businesses. Some are straightforward theft. The difference is not visible from a website, so here are the checks that do reveal it, applied to us as well as to everyone else.

Short answer

Some are legitimate and some are outright fraud, and the category as a whole is genuinely mixed, which is why the question keeps getting asked. Paying someone to help manage your appointment scheduling is not illegal, and a visible market of browser extensions, tracking tools, full-service visa apps and assistance services operates openly. But the same conditions that create the market, namely desperate applicants, long waits and an opaque portal, also make it attractive to people who simply take the money.

You cannot tell them apart by how professional the website looks. You can tell them apart by seven structural questions, below. The most important one: nobody, including us, can guarantee you an appointment slot or a visa. Any provider who does has already failed the test.

Why this category is genuinely mixed

Almost every market where people are desperate and information is scarce ends up with the same shape: a legitimate middle, a fraudulent fringe, and no easy way for a first-time buyer to see the line between them. US visa appointments have all three ingredients in unusual concentration.

The waits are long enough to break plans, over a year at some posts. The scheduling portal gives almost no explanation of its own behaviour. And the people searching are frequently under time pressure, often applying from a second country, and rarely repeat customers who might learn the market. That combination reliably produces both real services and predators.

It is worth saying plainly what the legitimate middle looks like, because "is this a scam" is often really asking "does anything here work at all". Slot-availability websites such as CheckVisaSlots and VisaGrader publish what the portal is showing and sell alert plans on top of it. Venture-funded visa platforms run whole applications end to end. Assistance services like ours work rescheduling cases. All of it operates in the open, on public websites, with published pricing and a company behind it. None of that is exotic or hidden, which is the useful test: the fraud has a completely different shape, and learning that shape is worth more than memorising any list of names.

Equally, the fraud is not hypothetical, and the US government has said so directly. The US Mission in India has warned publicly that no agent can guarantee a visa or an interview slot. That warning exists because operators were promising exactly that.

What the actual fraud looks like

Four patterns account for most of the harm. Knowing their shape is more useful than any blacklist, because the names change monthly and the mechanics do not.

Advance-fee theft

The simplest version. Full payment is demanded up front, usually to a personal bank account, a payment app or a crypto wallet, for an appointment that never materialises. Contact then stops. The structural giveaway is not the price. It is that all the money moves before any of the work.

Messaging-app operators with no business behind them

An account on a messaging platform, a display name, screenshots of confirmed appointments that may belong to anyone, and no website, no business email, no company. Screenshots are the least verifiable evidence that exists in this category and are trivially recycled between accounts.

Credential harvesting and blackmail

The most damaging pattern. The operator asks for your scheduling portal login "to check availability". That account contains your passport number, address, travel history and your entire DS-160. Documented cases exist of applicants being pressured for further payments using the personal data they handed over, and of appointments being altered or cancelled once access was granted.

Appointment hoarding

Slots booked in bulk against applicant details and then resold. Beyond the direct cost, this one can damage the applicant: appointments made improperly against your identity are a genuine problem to explain at a consulate, and the risk lands on you, not on the operator who sold it.

The one rule that filters most of this out

Money before work, and passwords before trust, are the two mechanisms every version of this fraud depends on. A structure where you pay only after a result, and where your portal login never leaves your own device, removes the leverage that makes the fraud profitable. Look for that structure first and judge the marketing second.

The seven checks

Run these before paying anyone in this category, including us. Each one is answerable in minutes, from public information, without contacting the provider.

1. Does it promise you a slot, a date, or a visa?

If yes, stop. Appointment availability depends on consular capacity and on other applicants cancelling. Nobody outside the US government controls either. Visa approval is decided by a consular officer on the day. A guarantee of either is a claim about something the provider does not control, which tells you what the rest of their claims are worth.

2. When do you pay, before or after a result?

Pay-on-success is not a marketing nicety, it is a structural safeguard. It puts the provider's incentive behind an outcome and means an operator who does nothing collects nothing. Full payment demanded up front, particularly to a personal account, is the precondition of the commonest fraud in this market.

3. Does it need your portal password?

Ask directly, and get the answer in writing. Arrangements where you keep your own login on your own device are meaningfully safer than handing credentials to a stranger. If a provider does require access, you should be able to read exactly who holds it, for how long, and what happens to it afterwards, in a published policy rather than a chat message.

4. Is the price stated in writing, before you commit?

A published fee that does not move is the baseline. Pricing that appears only in conversation, changes after you show interest, or grows once work is under way is a signal in itself.

5. Is there a written refund and cancellation policy?

Not a promise in a message, but a page on a website that existed before you arrived. It tells you what happens in the case both sides would rather not discuss, which is precisely the case worth knowing about.

6. Is there a contact channel that answers before you have paid?

Send a specific question about your consulate, your category and your timeline, then see what comes back. A real operation answers a pre-sales question with something specific. A harvesting operation answers with pressure, or with a payment link.

7. Can you identify who is behind it?

A named human being who is publicly attached to the business, a business email on the business domain, published terms and a privacy policy. This is the check most operators in this category fail, and it costs nothing to run: open the About page and see whether anyone is willing to put their name to it.

A note on reviews. Read recent reviews rather than the overall score, and distrust unanimity in both directions. Wholly positive review sets in a category this fraught are as suspicious as wholly negative ones, and a five-star average that stopped being updated a year ago describes a company that no longer exists in that form.

What a legitimate service can and cannot do

The clearest way to spot an operator who has drifted from the truth is to know exactly where the boundary sits.

Can: take the ongoing rescheduling work off your hands; know how a particular consulate's calendar tends to behave; act quickly and correctly when an earlier date becomes available; keep you from the errors that cost people their remaining reschedule attempts; explain what is actually happening when the portal offers no explanation at all.

Cannot: create appointments that do not exist; access any system the public cannot; influence a consular officer; guarantee a date, a timeframe or an outcome; make your application stronger. Anyone claiming a contact inside a consulate is describing either a fantasy or a crime, and you want no part of either.

The unglamorous truth is that every earlier date comes from the same place: another applicant cancelled or moved, and someone was positioned to take the slot. The service is diligence and attention, not access. Any pitch that implies otherwise is selling something that does not exist.

What to do if you have already been scammed

If you are reading this after the fact, move in this order.

  1. Stop sending money. A demand for a further fee to "release" or "confirm" an appointment is the second stage of the same fraud, not a path to recovery.
  2. Contact your bank or card issuer immediately and ask about a chargeback. Provide screenshots, payment references and the timeline. Card payments are far more recoverable than bank transfers, and transfers are far more recoverable than crypto.
  3. If you shared your portal login, change that password now, and check whether your appointment has been altered or cancelled. Change the password on your email account too if it shared that password.
  4. Report it. Your local cybercrime or consumer protection authority, and the messaging platform the account operated on. It rarely recovers the money but it does shorten the operator's runway.
  5. Verify your own application status directly in the official portal, not through anything the operator sent you. Screenshots prove nothing.

Frequently asked questions

Are US visa slot booking services legal?

Paying someone to help you manage the scheduling process is not itself illegal, and a large market of appointment-assistance services, slot-availability websites and full-service visa platforms operates openly. What is not permitted is anyone claiming to influence the visa decision, and what is outright fraud is taking payment for an appointment that is never delivered. The category is legal; individual operators within it vary enormously.

Can a visa agent guarantee me an appointment slot or a visa?

No. The US Mission in India has stated publicly that no agent can guarantee a visa or an interview slot. Appointment availability depends on consular capacity and on other applicants cancelling, neither of which any private company controls. Visa approval is decided solely by a consular officer. Any guarantee of either outcome is a reason to walk away.

Should I give a visa service my scheduling portal password?

Treat this as the single most important decision in the whole transaction. Your portal account holds your passport number, address, travel history and DS-160 data, which is everything needed for identity theft, and documented cases exist of applicants being blackmailed with data they handed over. Prefer arrangements where you keep your own login on your own device. If a provider requires your password, be certain you understand exactly who holds it and what their written retention policy says.

How much should a US visa appointment service cost?

Prices in this market commonly run from roughly $50 to a few hundred dollars per applicant, separate from the US$185 MRV fee that everybody pays to the government. Easy Visa Booking charges from $100, varying by consulate, payable only if an earlier date is secured. What matters more than the number is the structure: whether you pay before or after a result, and whether the price was stated in writing before you committed.

What are the warning signs of a US visa appointment scam?

The strongest signals are a guaranteed slot or guaranteed visa, full payment up front to a personal account or crypto wallet, contact only through a messaging app with no website or business email, pressure to decide immediately, a demand for your portal password with no written data policy, a claim of insider contacts at the consulate, and no published refund terms. Any single one of these justifies stopping.

Are free visa slot checking tools safe to use?

Slot-availability websites are widely used and are mostly ordinary software. They surface what the portal is showing and alert you; you still log in and book the slot yourself. Judge them the way you would judge any paid subscription: check whether they ask for your scheduling portal login, which no availability service needs, whether pricing and cancellation terms are published, and read recent reviews rather than the overall score, because behaviour in this category changes. Be clear about what you are buying. An alert is information, not an appointment, and you still have to be free to act on it within seconds.

What should I do if a visa appointment service has scammed me?

Stop paying immediately and send nothing further. Contact your bank or card issuer about a chargeback and provide the payment evidence. If you shared your portal login, change that password at once and check whether your appointment details were altered. Report the operator to your local cybercrime or consumer protection authority, and report the account to the messaging platform involved. Do not pay a further fee to recover an earlier one, which is a common second stage of the same fraud.

The bottom line

"Are these services legit" is the wrong shape of question, because the category contains both answers. The useful question is whether this specific provider passes seven checks that take ten minutes to run: no guarantees, payment after the result, no demand for your password, published pricing, written refund terms, a channel that answers, and a name attached to the business.

Run them on us. If we ever fail one of them, that is your answer, and you should act on it.

What nobody can promise you

Nobody outside the US government can guarantee an interview slot, a timeframe, or a visa decision. We cannot either, and we will not pretend otherwise to win your business. What we offer is that the work gets done properly, that you pay only if it produces a result, and that your portal login stays with you.

Can You Apply for a US Visa in Another Country? What Changed in September 2025

Flying somewhere with a shorter queue was the standard answer to a long wait for more than a decade. It was never banned outright, and it stopped being a good idea on 6 September 2025.

M

Megh

Founder, Easy Visa Booking · 19 August 2026 ·11 min read

Short answer

You can, but since 6 September 2025 you are directed not to, and the consequences of trying fall entirely on you. The State Department instructs nonimmigrant visa applicants to schedule at the post in their country of nationality or legal residence, and warns those who go elsewhere that they may find it harder to qualify. Fees are neither refunded nor transferred. It is guidance rather than a ban, which is exactly what makes it dangerous: nothing stops you booking, and nothing protects you when it fails.

What changed, precisely

Third country national processing, to use the term the immigration bar uses, meant applying for a US visa somewhere that was neither your home country nor your country of residence. It was ordinary practice for decades, and for applicants facing a queue measured in months it was often the only sensible move.

On 6 September 2025, effective immediately, the State Department published guidance directing nonimmigrant visa applicants to schedule at the post in their country of nationality or residence. An update followed on 10 October 2025.

Three features of how it was written decide everything downstream:

  • It is guidance, not a prohibition. No outright restriction was imposed, and the systems will still let you book. That is the trap. A rule that blocks you costs you nothing; guidance that discourages you costs you the fee.
  • Existing appointments were generally not cancelled. Anyone already booked at a third country post before the announcement was not swept out, which is why you still hear about people who did it successfully. They booked before.
  • The warning is about qualifying, not about scheduling. The department said applicants who schedule outside their country of nationality or residence might find it more difficult to qualify for the visa. That is a warning about the interview, not the calendar, and it is the part people skip.

What "country of residence" actually means

The guidance has two limbs, nationality and residence, and the second one is where most real cases sit.

Residence means where you actually live, on a status that permits it. The State Department states that applicants applying in their country of residence rather than their country of nationality must be prepared to demonstrate that residence. Consular practice varies, but the evidence that satisfies this question is the ordinary evidence of a life somewhere:

  • A residence permit, long-term visa or equivalent status document
  • An employment contract or a business registration, with pay records
  • A tenancy agreement or property ownership, and utility accounts in your name
  • Local bank activity over a period, not a freshly opened account
  • Family living with you, school enrolment, a driving licence

What does not make you resident: a tourist entry, a short business trip, a visitor visa for the country you are standing in, or an address borrowed from a relative. This distinction is the whole test, and the number of applicants who assume a few weeks in a country counts is the reason it needs stating.

Who can still legitimately apply abroad

Three groups, and if you are in one of them the news here is good rather than bad.

Expatriates applying where they live

This is the largest group by far and it is not an exception, it is the rule working as intended. An Indian national resident in the UAE applies in the UAE. A Filipino national resident in Canada applies in Canada. A British national resident in Singapore applies in Singapore. Your residence is your post, and the only new obligation is being ready to demonstrate it.

Nationals of countries without routine visa operations

Where the US is not conducting routine nonimmigrant visa operations, applicants are directed to a designated post instead, unless their residence is elsewhere. That is a specific mechanism rather than a free choice of country, so check what has been designated for your nationality rather than picking the nearest convenient post.

Categories outside the guidance

A, G, C-2, C-3 and NATO categories, and diplomatic and official visas, sit outside it. There is also limited allowance for exceptions on humanitarian or medical emergency grounds, or for foreign policy reasons. If you are reading this page because of a family medical emergency, that route exists and it is worth asking the post about directly.

Why it is a bad bet for everyone else

And weigh that against what you were escaping. If your post at home is six months out and the alternative post is one month out, you are spending several thousand dollars and accepting a stated qualification risk to save five months on a queue you could have kept working from home for nothing.

The four routes that closed

Each of these was a genuine strategy before September 2025 and each has an ecosystem of old advice still describing it as current.

  • India to the UAE. The biggest of them by volume. Fly to Dubai or Abu Dhabi, interview there, return. The UAE is now the post for people who live in the UAE, and the mission has spent much of 2026 running reduced operations besides.
  • India and elsewhere to Canada. Once a well worn route, and now doubly unattractive: the guidance applies, and Canadian visitor visa queues are among the longest in the world, with Toronto sitting near 495 days.
  • Latin America to Mexico. Still popular in advice written before the guidance, and now carrying the same warning as everywhere else. That Mexico participates in the $750 expedite pilot makes it more tempting, not more available.
  • South and Southeast Asia to Thailand. A long standing regional route, and subject to exactly the same direction.

If you find a guide that recommends any of these without mentioning September 2025, you have found a page that has not been updated in a year. Check the date on anything you read about this, including this page.

What to do instead

The exit being closed does not leave you with nothing. It leaves you with the same four levers as everyone else, and they are worth more attention now than they used to get.

  1. Book any date at your own post, immediately. A distant confirmed appointment is the asset everything else depends on. You cannot reschedule what you do not hold.
  2. Check every post in your own country. Intra-country spreads are enormous and almost nobody uses them. India ran from about 60 days at Kolkata to about 315 at Hyderabad in August 2026. Australia ran from about 15 days at Perth to about 435 at Sydney. Those are legitimate moves inside the guidance, and they are larger than most people expect.
  3. Widen the range of dates you will accept. The single biggest lever you control, and it costs nothing.
  4. Use the expedite request if you genuinely qualify. Free, discretionary, decided on documented urgency. Medical emergencies, funerals of immediate family, unavoidable urgent business travel. Not a holiday.

Notice that the second one is the closest legitimate substitute for what third country processing used to do. You are still moving to a shorter queue, you are just doing it inside your own country, where nobody has advised you not to.

Frequently asked questions

Is it illegal to apply for a US visa in another country?

No, and that distinction matters. The State Department issued guidance, not a prohibition. Applicants are directed to schedule at the post in their country of nationality or legal residence, and existing third country appointments were generally not cancelled. What changed is that the department warned applicants who schedule elsewhere that they might find it more difficult to qualify for the visa. It is still possible, it is simply a much worse bet than it was.

When did third country US visa processing change?

On 6 September 2025, effective immediately, with an update published on 10 October 2025. Before that date, applying at a post outside your home country was a well established route, and the UAE, Canada, Mexico and Thailand were all heavily used by applicants escaping long queues at home.

What counts as my country of residence?

Where you actually live, on a status that allows it, rather than where you happen to be travelling. The State Department states that applicants applying in their country of residence rather than their country of nationality must be prepared to demonstrate that residence. In practice that means a residence permit or long-term visa, and the ordinary evidence of a life there: a tenancy or property, employment, bank activity, family. A tourist entry does not make you resident anywhere.

Do I get my fee back if I apply in the wrong country?

No. The MRV fee is non-refundable and non-transferable, and the guidance states plainly that fees paid are neither refunded nor transferred between posts. That is the financial core of this: you pay the fee, book the flights and accommodation, and if the plan does not work none of it comes back. Layering an optional expedite fee on top only increases what is at risk.

Who is outside this guidance?

It does not apply to applicants for A, G, C-2, C-3 or NATO visas, or to diplomatic and official categories. There is also limited allowance for exceptions on humanitarian or medical emergency grounds, and for foreign policy reasons. Separately, nationals of countries where the US is not conducting routine nonimmigrant visa operations are directed to a designated post, unless their residence is somewhere else.

I live abroad on a work permit. Which post do I use?

The one in the country where you live. That is the whole point of the residence limb of the guidance, and for expatriates it is the reassuring half of the news: an Indian national resident in the UAE applies in the UAE, and a Filipino national resident in Canada applies in Canada. Be ready to show the residence, because you may be asked to.

Can I still fly to Canada to use the $750 paid expedite?

Not usefully. The expedite fee buys an earlier interview at a participating mission, currently Mission Mexico and Mission Canada, but it does not change where you are supposed to apply. An applicant resident elsewhere who books in Canada and pays $750 has spent the application fee and the expedite fee, neither of which is refundable, on an appointment the department has already advised against. It is the most expensive version of this mistake.

The bottom line

Third country processing was not banned. It was discouraged, in writing, with a warning attached about qualifying and an explicit statement that your fees do not follow you. For a rule you can still technically ignore, that is about as clear as governments get.

If you live somewhere, that somewhere is your post, and the guidance is not aimed at you. If you were planning to fly to a shorter queue, price the whole thing honestly: fee, flights, accommodation, time off, and a stated risk that the interview goes worse than it would have at home, against a queue that is still sitting there when you get back.

The closest legitimate substitute is the one almost nobody uses. Check every post inside your own country. The gaps are bigger than you think, and using them costs you a domestic flight rather than an international one.

What nobody can promise you

Nobody outside the US government can guarantee an interview slot, a timeframe, or a visa decision. We cannot either, and we cannot tell you how a particular consular officer will treat a particular case. What we offer is that the work gets done properly, at the post you are supposed to use, and that you pay only if it produces a result.

The single most useful thing to understand is that when an intermediary breaks the scheduling rules, the sanction lands on the applicant. The applicants lost the date, the account and the fee. The people who took the money were not the ones sanctioned.

Who actually carries the loss

Most scam guides in this category count the money and stop there. The money is usually the smaller loss.

On 26 March 2025 the US Mission in India announced it had identified appointments booked through automated means, cancelled roughly 2,000 of them, and suspended the scheduling privileges of the associated accounts. The mission described zero tolerance for intermediaries violating scheduling policy.

Read the consequences carefully, because they are not distributed the way people assume:

  • The appointment is cancelled. At a post with a queue measured in hundreds of days, that is not an inconvenience. It can be the trip.
  • The account is restricted. The scheduling privileges suspended belonged to the applicants, so the ability to book again is affected too.
  • The fee is gone. The MRV fee is non-refundable and non-transferable, and whatever was paid to the intermediary is not coming back either.
  • The intermediary is fine. They still have their money and can open another channel the same afternoon.

This asymmetry is the whole reason to be careful about method rather than only about price. A cheap service that gets your account sanctioned has cost you far more than an expensive one that does not.

The seven live patterns in 2026

1. Advance-fee slot selling

The classic, and still the most common. Someone in a messaging group offers an earlier interview date for a fee, with screenshots of appointment confirmations as proof. Screenshots are trivially fabricated and prove nothing.

Reported cases in India follow a consistent shape: a channel with thousands of members, a fee in the region of ten thousand rupees, a payment, a confirmation that turns out to be fabricated, a demand for a second payment, and then the applicant locked out of their own scheduling account. The second demand is the design, not an escalation. It works because by then the victim has already paid once and wants to believe.

2. Selling the $750 expedite where it does not exist

New in 2026 and unusually effective, because the underlying thing is real. The State Department does run a pilot letting B-1/B-2 applicants pay $750 for an interview within ten business days. It runs only at designated missions, which as of 19 August 2026 means Mission Mexico and Mission Canada.

Anyone offering it for a post outside that list is selling something that does not exist there. And note what that means for recovery: there is no government fee to be refunded and no official receipt to dispute, because no transaction ever touched the government. Where the pilot does apply, the fee is paid inside your own scheduling account, never to an intermediary.

3. Fake demands for the $250 visa integrity fee

Also new, and well designed. The $250 visa integrity fee is genuine, it is charged at the point your visa is issued rather than when you apply, and its collection has rolled out unevenly across posts. So an applicant who receives a demand for $250 has no easy way to tell whether it is the real thing arriving at an unusual moment.

The rule is simple: the fee is collected by the US government through your own consular process. It does not arrive as a payment link in a message, it is not collected by an agent, and it is never paid to a personal account. If a demand did not come from your own consular process, it is not the integrity fee.

4. Look-alike scheduling domains

Not every country uses the same scheduling website, and the map has changed recently. India, Canada and many others use the AIS portal at ais.usvisa-info.com. The UAE and others moved to usvisascheduling.com in 2025. Applicants therefore cannot rely on recognising the right site by sight, and search results and old bookmarks are both unreliable.

That uncertainty is what impersonation sites exploit, and the payment page is where they pay off. Reach your scheduling site by following the link from your own embassy or consulate's website every time.

5. Offers to book you in another country

Once a genuine strategy, now mostly a way to lose money. Since the State Department's guidance of 6 September 2025, updated 10 October 2025, nonimmigrant visa applicants are directed to apply in their country of nationality or legal residence, and those who schedule elsewhere are told to expect significantly longer waits and a harder case, with fees neither refunded nor transferred.

An operator still selling third country appointments as a shortcut is either not following the guidance or not telling you about it. Either way the fee is yours to lose.

6. Guaranteed approval

Persistent in large expatriate markets, where a first-time applicant may have no idea how adjudication works. Nobody outside the US government influences a visa decision. A consular officer applies the standard to your evidence and that is the whole process. An offer of guaranteed approval is not an exaggeration of a real service, it is a description of something nobody can do.

Its close relative is guaranteed documents: fabricated bank statements, employment letters or itineraries. That one does not just cost money, it exposes you to a misrepresentation finding that can follow you for life.

7. Data harvesting and the follow-up

The quietest pattern and the one with the longest tail. A DS-160 contains your passport details, address history, employment, family members, travel history and more. An operator that collects your documents and your login has a complete identity package whether or not they ever book anything.

What follows is sometimes a second approach months later from a different name, sometimes pressure using what they hold. Before you hand over documents to anybody, the questions are what is collected, where it is held, how long it is kept and how you have it deleted, and the answers should be in a published policy rather than a reassuring message.

What a legitimate service looks like instead

Fraud guides are long on warnings and short on what "fine" looks like, which leaves readers unable to tell a real business from a careful scam. Six things a real one has, all of them checkable before you pay:

  • A price stated in public. On the website, before any conversation. A price that only appears after you describe your urgency is a price set by your desperation.
  • Payment terms you can read. Written terms, a refund policy and a privacy policy, published, not summarised in a chat.
  • A named human and a real support channel. Someone accountable, and a way to reach them that is not a single anonymous messaging handle.
  • A written statement of what it cannot do. Any operator honest about this process says plainly that no date and no decision can be promised. Its absence is more telling than any promise.
  • A straight answer about method. You are entitled to ask how the work is done, because the consequences of the wrong answer land on your account.
  • Willingness to tell you not to pay. A service that never says "your case does not need us" is selling to everyone who arrives.

If you have already paid

Order matters here. Most people go for the money first, and the money is usually the least recoverable part.

  1. Secure the account. Change your scheduling account password immediately, and change the email address on it if that has been altered. Check that your appointment still exists and that the contact details are yours.
  2. Check your DS-160. Confirm the confirmation number linked to your appointment is the one you submitted, and that nothing in the application has been edited.
  3. Tell the official scheduling support channel. For your country, reached from your embassy or consulate's own website. If your account has been compromised they need to know, and disclosing it yourself is better than it surfacing later.
  4. Report the fraud. To your local police, and to the US embassy or consulate that will adjudicate your application. The State Department publishes where to send reports of visa fraud committed overseas.
  5. Then pursue the money. Card payments have some chance through your issuer. Bank transfers to personal accounts and payments through messaging apps are rarely recoverable, which is precisely why they are the requested methods.
  6. Do not pay again to "fix" it. The second demand is part of the design. Nothing gets unlocked by paying more.

The bottom line

Fraud in this category works because the underlying problem is genuine. Waits at some posts run past a year, the consequences of missing a trip are real, and an offer of a date next month is exactly what a worried applicant wants to hear.

Two facts protect you from almost all of it. Nothing can be reserved before you have an account, a DS-160 and a paid fee, so a slot offered in advance does not exist. And every government fee in this process is paid to the government inside your own consular process, never to an intermediary.

The third is the one to remember longest, because it is the one nobody tells you: when a shortcut breaks the scheduling rules, it is your appointment that is cancelled and your account that is suspended. Two thousand people in India learned that at once. How the work is done is your risk, which is why you are entitled to a straight answer about it before you pay anyone.

Short answer

The visa integrity fee is $250, it is charged when your visa is issued rather than when you apply, and it takes the real cost of a US visitor visa from $185 to $435 per person. It was created by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 4 July 2025, it covers almost every nonimmigrant category including dependants, and you are not charged it if you are refused. Collection is still uneven across consulates, so whether you pay it today depends on your post.

What the fee is, and where it came from

Almost every article about US visa costs still quotes one number, $185, and stops. That number has been incomplete since the middle of 2025 and the gap is now $250 a head.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed on 4 July 2025 as Public Law 119-21, created a visa integrity fee. The mechanics that matter:

  • $250 is the base, not the ceiling. The Department of Homeland Security can set it higher by regulation, and from fiscal year 2026 it rises annually with the Consumer Price Index. It is designed to drift upwards.
  • It is charged at issuance. The State Department collects it when your visa is actually issued, which is after the interview and after approval. That is a genuinely different point in the process from every other fee you have paid.
  • It applies per person, across almost every category. B-1/B-2, F-1, H-1B, L-1, O-1, TN and the rest, and it reaches dependants too: an H-4 spouse, an L-2 child and an F-2 dependant each owe their own $250.
  • It is additional. It does not replace the MRV application fee, the SEVIS fee for students, or any reciprocity fee your nationality attracts.

One thing about it is unambiguously good for applicants, and it is worth saying plainly because most coverage of this fee is uniformly negative. Because it lands at issuance, a refused applicant does not pay it. That is the opposite of how the MRV fee works, and in a process where refusal is common it matters.

The arithmetic that catches families out: none of it is per household. Four B-1/B-2 applicants are $740 in application fees and $1,000 in integrity fees, so $1,740 before a single flight, document or photograph. Where the old figure of $185 each made a family application feel like a manageable $740, the true number is more than double that.

Is it being charged yet? The honest answer

This is the question every applicant actually has, and it is the one almost no page answers straight, because the straight answer is uncomfortable: it depends which consulate you are dealing with.

The statutory start was 1 October 2025, the beginning of fiscal year 2026. What followed was not a single switch-on. Practitioner reporting through the first half of 2026 describes an uneven rollout, with some posts actively collecting the fee while others were still waiting for payment and tracking systems to go live. University international offices, which see large volumes of student cases across many posts and have no reason to overstate any of this, were reporting in May 2026 that some posts had begun charging it while others had not.

So the practical position in the second half of 2026:

  • Budget the full $435. If you are not charged, you are $250 better off than planned. Planning the other way round is how a trip gets cancelled at the last step.
  • Take your instructions from your own post, at the time. Not from an article, this one included, and not from a forum thread about a different country. The consulate issuing your visa is the only authority on what it will charge you.
  • Keep proof of payment. If a refund mechanism ever appears, the people who kept a receipt will be the only ones able to use it.
  • Pay it only where the post tells you to. A new fee with an inconsistent rollout is an unusually good cover story for fraud. See the warning below.

The fee is a scam vector, and a good one

Think about what this fee looks like from a fraudster's point of view. It is real, it is widely reported, it is genuinely charged at some posts and not others, and it is payable at an unusual point in the process. That combination means a demand for $250 arriving by email or message is both plausible and hard for an applicant to check. Nobody outside the US government collects this fee. It is not paid to an agent, not to a personal bank account, not through a payment link in a message, and not to anyone offering to "clear" it for you. If a demand does not come from your own consular process, it is not the integrity fee.

The refund almost nobody will collect

The statute does allow the fee to come back to you. The conditions are that you do not work without authorisation and that you either leave within five days of your authorised stay ending or obtain a lawful extension or change of status.

Read that in the real world and the picture changes.

  • There is no way to claim it. No portal, no form and no published instructions have appeared from the Department of Homeland Security or the State Department. A right with no mechanism is not a right you can exercise.
  • The clock is very long. A ten-year B-1/B-2 means the earliest a claim could even arise is a decade after payment, on the basis of compliance evidence you would have to have kept for that whole period.
  • The compliance bar is exacting. Five days is a narrow window, and proving a clean record of it years later is a documentation problem most travellers will not have solved.

Our advice is unglamorous and we would rather give it than a hopeful one: treat the $250 as spent. Keep the receipt, because it costs nothing to keep, but do not build any part of a travel budget on getting it back.

Who does not pay it

Two groups are outside the fee, and one of them is larger than people realise.

Visa Waiver Program travellers

If your nationality lets you travel on an ESTA rather than a visa, the integrity fee does not touch you. The ESTA itself did get more expensive: it rose from $21 to $40 for applications filed on or after 30 September 2025, and an inflation adjustment took it to $40.27 in January 2026. That is still an order of magnitude below $435, and it is the single biggest cost difference between the two routes into the United States.

Diplomatic and official categories

A and G visa categories, and a small number of related classifications, sit outside it. For anybody reading this page that is unlikely to be the relevant exemption.

Everyone else is in scope, and the point worth repeating is that it reaches dependants. An H-1B worker moving a spouse and two children is looking at four integrity fees, not one.

No fee here buys you a faster interview

Because the integrity fee arrived at the same time as a genuine paid fast lane, the two get confused constantly, and the confusion costs people money. They are unrelated.

The bottom line

The honest headline number for a US visitor visa in 2026 is $435 per person, not $185. Most of the internet has not caught up, our own older pages included, and the gap is not small: a family of four is out by a thousand dollars.

The fee has one genuinely fair feature, which is that a refusal does not attract it. It has one feature that is fair on paper only, which is the refund, and until a claim mechanism exists you should treat that money as gone. And it has one feature that will cost people who do not read carefully, which is that the uneven rollout makes a fake demand for $250 unusually believable.

None of it buys you a faster interview. The only fee that does that is the $750 pilot, in two countries, for one visa category, until the end of December. Everywhere else, an earlier date is still made of rescheduling and persistence, and that has not changed at all.

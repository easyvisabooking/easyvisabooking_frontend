Short answer

In August 2026 the interview-required US visitor visa wait in India ran from about 60 days at Kolkata to about 315 days at Hyderabad, with Mumbai and New Delhi both near 255. Same country, same portal, same rules, roughly eight months apart. India also allows only one reschedule per MRV fee, so the post you apply at and the single move you make matter more here than almost anywhere.

Three things fall out of that table immediately.

  • Kolkata is the outlier and nobody uses it. Around 60 days against 315 at Hyderabad is not a marginal difference. It is the difference between travelling this year and travelling next.
  • The visitor queue is the bad queue. At Mumbai and New Delhi a student wait was roughly a third of the visitor wait and a petition-based wait roughly a sixth. If you are in one of those categories the headline number is not your problem.
  • Hyderabad is worst on every line. It carries the longest visitor wait and the longest student and worker waits too, which tells you the constraint there is capacity rather than category mix.

Why one country has an eight month spread

Nothing in the rules differs between Indian posts. Same scheduling platform, same fee, same reschedule allowance, same DS-160. A wait time is just staffing divided by demand, and both vary sharply across a country of this size.

Hyderabad sits at the centre of a region that generates enormous petition-based and student volume, and every one of those interviews consumes capacity that could otherwise go to visitor cases. Mumbai and New Delhi are the two largest posts and absorb the broadest mix. Kolkata serves a smaller catchment with lighter visitor demand, and the result shows up in its calendar.

The self-reinforcing part is what makes it usable. Applicants gravitate to the post they know, which concentrates demand where it already is and leaves the quieter post quieter. Kolkata is short partly because most people never look at it.

Check the consular district before you plan around Kolkata

We are not going to pretend that "just book Kolkata" is a universal answer, because it is not. Which Indian post you may use is governed by consular district rules and by what your scheduling account actually offers you, and those do not always follow the map you would expect. Some applicants will find the option is simply not there. Check what your account offers before you build a plan on a number from a table, including this one.

One reschedule changes how you should play this

This is the part that makes India genuinely different, and most advice written for a global audience gets it wrong.

Since 1 January 2025 an Indian applicant has had one reschedule per MRV fee. Several countries allow three. Japan has allowed six. With six attempts you can take a small improvement now and keep improving. With one, every move is final until you pay another fee.

Practical consequences, in order of how much they cost people:

  1. Do not spend it on a small gain. Moving from a date in July to one in late June feels like progress and ends your ability to reach May. Decide in advance what improvement is worth your single attempt, and hold to it.
  2. Take the first date you are offered when you book. Whatever it is. Your one attempt only exists if you hold a confirmed appointment, and a distant date you can move beats no date at all.
  3. Watch the MRV clock. The fee is valid for a year from payment and your interview has to be scheduled inside that window. An unused attempt is worth nothing once the receipt has expired.
  4. Do not test the system. There is no dry run. An attempt is consumed by a completed change, and there is no undo.

The Dubai and Toronto route is closed

For years the answer to an Indian queue was to leave it. Applicants flew to Dubai, Abu Dhabi, Toronto or Bangkok, interviewed there, and came home with a visa while the Indian calendar stayed full. Whole businesses were built on it.

That changed on 6 September 2025, with an update on 10 October 2025. The State Department directs nonimmigrant visa applicants to schedule at the post in their country of nationality or legal residence. It is not an outright prohibition, and existing third country appointments were generally not cancelled, but applicants who schedule elsewhere are warned they may find it more difficult to qualify for the visa, and fees already paid are neither refunded nor transferred.

Read the risk honestly. You would be paying for flights and accommodation, applying at a post where the department has told you the case is harder, with no refund if it does not work, in order to escape a queue you could have kept working from home for nothing. For an applicant resident in India, this is not a shortcut any more.

The narrow exceptions do exist: limited allowance for humanitarian or medical emergencies, and the guidance does not apply to A, G, C-2, C-3 and NATO categories. If you genuinely live abroad rather than merely travelling there, your country of residence is your post, and you should be prepared to demonstrate that residence.

What actually shortens an Indian wait

Four things, in the order they are worth your effort.

  • Widen your acceptable date range. The biggest single lever, and it costs nothing. Hunting one specific week is a much harder problem than accepting anything inside a two month band, and with one reschedule attempt the wide range is what makes that attempt likely to land somewhere worth having.
  • Check whether a quieter post is open to you. Kolkata at 60 days against Hyderabad at 315 is the largest single improvement available to anyone whose district rules allow it.
  • Work cancellations, not bulk releases. At posts with waits this long, the realistic supply is appointments returning to the pool when other people move theirs. That rewards steady checking rather than any clever hour.
  • Use the expedite request if, and only if, you qualify. It is free, it is discretionary, and it is decided on documented urgency: medical emergencies, the funeral of an immediate family member, unavoidable urgent business travel, documented student start dates. A holiday is not urgency, and a weak request tends to burn the one credible attempt you had.

Notice what is not on that list. There is no paid government fast lane in India: the State Department's $750 expedited appointment pilot runs at Mission Mexico and Mission Canada, and no Indian post participates.

The bottom line

India does not have one wait time, it has five, and in August 2026 they were about eight months apart end to end. Before you accept the number for the post you assumed you would use, find out which posts your application can actually reach.

Then treat the single reschedule as what it is: one decision, not a process. Widen the range of dates you will accept as far as your plans genuinely allow, because that is what turns one attempt into a useful one. And take the first date the system offers when you book, because everything else on this page depends on holding an appointment.

What is no longer available is the exit. Flying to Dubai or Toronto to escape an Indian queue was the standard answer for a decade and it stopped being sensible in September 2025. The queue is the queue now, and it is worked from where you live.

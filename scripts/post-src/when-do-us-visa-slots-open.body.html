Short answer

There is no worldwide release time. Each mission sets its own, a few publish it in writing, and most do not. Mission UAE releases nonimmigrant interview appointments every Friday morning between 7:30 and 11:30. The US Embassy in the Dominican Republic opens new B-1/B-2 appointments every Wednesday at 8 a.m. Where no schedule is published, the supply you are realistically competing for is cancellations, which return at no fixed hour.

The two supplies, and why it matters which one you are chasing

Almost every argument online about "when slots open" is two people describing different things. Appointments reach the calendar by two routes, and they behave nothing alike.

Bulk release Cancellations
What it is The post loads a new block of interview dates onto the calendar Somebody else moves or drops an appointment and it returns to the pool
Timing Scheduled. Sometimes weekly, sometimes monthly, occasionally published Continuous, all day, no pattern worth modelling
Volume Many dates at once, gone within minutes at a busy post One at a time, all day long
Who wins it Whoever is already signed in when the window opens Whoever happens to look in the right minute
Realistic for you? Only if you know the window and can be there Yes, and this is where most earlier dates actually come from

Behaviour is described from what missions publish and from how the scheduling platforms present availability. Individual posts vary, and none of it is guaranteed.

The practical consequence: if your post has a queue measured in hundreds of days, chasing bulk releases is chasing an event that may happen once a month and be over in minutes. Cancellations are the supply you can actually reach, and they reward steady, unglamorous checking rather than any clever hour of the day.

Missions that publish their release window

This is the part almost nothing online tells you, and it is easy to verify: some missions state their release schedule on their own website. Two current examples, both from the posts themselves rather than from a forum.

Mission Published release window Exceptions stated
UAE: Abu Dhabi and Dubai Every Friday morning, 7:30 a.m. to 11:30 a.m. US and UAE holidays. Additional appointments may be released outside the schedule
Dominican Republic Every Wednesday at 8 a.m., B-1/B-2, subject to availability US and Dominican holidays

As published by each mission and retrieved 19 August 2026. Missions change these without announcement, so treat the mission's own page as the authority and this table as a pointer to it.

Two things follow from those examples, and they are worth more than any timing theory.

  • Release is scheduled, not random. Where a post has told us, it is a fixed weekday inside working hours. That is a strong reason to disbelieve any advice built on unpredictability or on the middle of the night.
  • It is a working-hours, working-day event. Both published windows sit in the local morning and both skip holidays. Whatever your post does, it is far more likely to look like that than like 2 a.m. on a Sunday.

So the first thing to do is not to look for a universal answer. It is to read your own mission's visa pages and its scheduling site's announcements section. Most say nothing. The minority that say something have handed you the most valuable fact available.

A published window is public, which cuts both ways

If your mission publishes its release schedule, every applicant reading the same page knows it too. That makes the published window the most contested period of the week at that post, and it is why we will not tell you that knowing the timing is the whole trick. It removes the guesswork. It does not remove the queue, and at a post running reduced operations a scheduled window can pass with almost nothing in it.

What to do when your post publishes nothing

Which is most of them. In that case you are working the cancellation supply, and the honest advice is simple even though it is not satisfying.

The State Department itself sets the expectation on its wait times pages: posts regularly release additional appointment slots, and applicants who want an earlier date after scheduling should check back frequently and move to another slot if one is available. That is the official method. It is also, notably, an admission that the first date you are given is not the best one available to you over time.

What that looks like in practice:

  1. Hold a confirmed appointment first. Nothing here works without one. Rescheduling moves a booking; it does not create one.
  2. Know your attempt budget. If your country allows one change per MRV fee, you are not looking for any earlier date, you are looking for a good one. If it allows six, you can afford to take an improvement and improve again.
  3. Decide your acceptable range before you look. The single biggest factor in whether this works is how wide a window you will accept. Hunting one specific morning is a different and much harder problem than accepting anything inside a two month band.
  4. Check on a rhythm, not continuously. See the routine below.

The midnight myth, and three others

"Slots drop at midnight"

The most durable belief in this subject, and the least supported. It survives because scheduling systems do housekeeping around date boundaries, so screens refresh, counters reset and availability appears to change without a single new interview being loaded. Where release schedules are actually published they are mid-morning on a working day. Staying up for midnight costs you sleep and buys nothing.

"Fridays are always best"

True in the UAE, because the mission published it. Not a rule you can carry to another country. The lesson from the UAE is not that Friday is special, it is that the post decides and sometimes tells you.

"Clearing cookies or switching browsers reveals hidden slots"

There are no hidden slots. The calendar shows what is loaded and available to your case. What repeated aggressive reloading does reliably produce is a temporary restriction on your account.

"Some accounts get better availability"

Availability differs by visa category, by post and by whether your fee and DS-160 are in order. It does not differ by account seniority or by anything you can influence. If a friend at the same post sees dates you cannot, check the category and the post before concluding anything about your account.

Patterns that do hold

Four things are worth acting on, and none of them is a secret hour.

  • Working days at the post, not yours. Movement happens when consular staff are working. If the post's weekend is Friday and Saturday, its quiet days are not the same as yours.
  • The days after a closure. Holidays compress work and produce cancellations from people whose plans changed. A published window that skips a holiday often means a larger release next time.
  • Local mornings. Both published windows are morning windows, and cancellations cluster in working hours because that is when applicants are acting on their own bookings.
  • Both posts in your country, not just the near one. Consular sections in the same country hold separate calendars and can differ by months. In Australia the spread between Sydney and Perth has run to well over a year. Applicants who only ever check the convenient post are the ones who wait longest.

A checking routine that will not get you restricted

The tension in this whole subject is that checking more often finds more openings, and checking too often gets you locked out for a day or three. A workable middle:

  1. Three or four deliberate checks a day, spread out. One in the post's morning, one around midday, one in its late afternoon. Sign in, look, sign out.
  2. If your post publishes a window, be signed in before it opens. Signing in is the slow part and it is the part you can do in advance.
  3. Never sit on a reload loop. Continuous refreshing is what triggers the temporary restriction, and the restriction costs you far more checking time than you gained.
  4. If pages stop loading, stop for a day. That is the restriction, not an outage. It clears. Changing browser, device or network to keep going is repeating the behaviour that caused it.
  5. Keep your deadline in view. Beyond a point, this stops being a search and becomes a habit that eats your week. If you are three weeks in and nothing has moved, the answer is probably not more checking.

The bottom line

There is no universal release time, and the confident articles that give you one are describing a single country or repeating a forum post. What is real is that release is configured mission by mission, that a minority of missions publish their schedule, and that at everywhere else the supply you can actually reach is cancellations.

So the useful sequence is short. Read your own mission's pages first, because if it publishes a window nothing else on this page matters as much. If it does not, work the cancellation supply on a sustainable rhythm, widen your acceptable date range as far as your plans allow, and check both posts in your country rather than the convenient one.

And stop refreshing at midnight. Nobody is loading appointments then.
